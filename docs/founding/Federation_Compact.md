# The Federation Compact

*A reference draft, offered as the treaty between Triarchal deployments. It has no parties yet — it activates once more than one deployment has ratified it (7.1) — and it will be amended or rewritten by the deployments that sign it. Published now so that every future deployment knows, before it builds, what the lattice asks and what it never asks.*

*Status: one deployment is publicly documented today — the Triarc, the nonprofit that develops and stewards Triarchal Framework and governs its own operations directly. It runs as its own independent instance, with its own Worker, database, and `communityId`. This compact remains dormant until more than one deployment ratifies it.*

---

## Preamble

Triarchal Democracy is designed to work alongside government at every level, deployed by any community that chooses it. No central authority holds governance power over any deployment. A federation of self-governing communities needs shared minimums, mutual recognition, and a way to resolve disputes between equals. It does not need a capital, and this compact refuses to become one.

## Article I — Parties and autonomy

**1.1** A party to this compact is any deployment of Triarchal Democracy — online network, municipality, cooperative, or other community — that ratifies it through its own governance process.

**1.2** Every party is sovereign over its own governance. Nothing in this compact grants any party, any council of parties, or any foundation — including the Triarc — authority over another party's decisions, treasury, membership, or rules. The compact creates standards and recognition, never sovereignty.

**1.3** The compact is indifferent to outcomes and vigilant only about structure.

## Article II — The minimums

**2.1** To be recognised under this compact, a deployment's governing documents must guarantee, at minimum:

- Every binding decision: one member, one vote — with influence flowing only through voluntary, revocable, renewal-cycled delegation
- A public, permanent, append-only governance record
- Standing and votes that cannot be purchased, inherited, or transferred
- Member rights no weaker than the Member Bill of Rights, including voice, due process, graduated sanctions, identity protection, and free exit
- Dispute resolution by randomly selected member panels
- A transparent treasury governed only by ratified community decision

**2.2** The minimums are a floor, never a ceiling. Parties may exceed them in any direction their communities choose.

**2.3** Recognition is verified against a party's published documents and public record — the same materials any member of the public can read. There are no inspectors; there is only the record.

## Article III — The commons

**3.1** Triarchal Framework is open source under the GNU Affero General Public License v3.0. Every party may deploy, modify, and extend it freely. Improvements that any party deems generally useful are contributed back under that same licence.

**3.2** No party is obligated to run any other party's code, accept any other party's amendments, or upgrade on any schedule but its own.

## Article IV — The name

**4.1** The name "Triarchal Democracy," and the marks associated with it, may be used by any party meeting the minimums of Article II. "The Triarc" is not available under this grant: it is the proper name of the nonprofit that develops and stewards Triarchal Framework, and of its own governing deployment. Each deployment's own proper name belongs to that deployment alone.

**4.2** A party whose documents or conduct fall below the minimums loses the name, not its independence. It keeps its code, its treasury, its records, and its right to govern itself however its members choose, and may no longer call the result Triarchal Democracy. Determinations under this article are made by a joint panel under Article V, never by any single party or foundation.

## Article V — Disputes between parties

**5.1** Disputes between parties — over recognition, the name, the commons, or the compact itself — go to a **joint panel**: eleven members drawn by verifiable public randomness from the dispute pools of parties uninvolved in the dispute, no two panelists from the same deployment.

**5.2** Joint panels follow the Dispute Procedure Code's timelines and standards, adapted by agreement of the parties to the dispute. Decisions are published in the records of all parties.

**5.3** While fewer than eleven uninvolved parties exist, disputes are resolved by direct negotiation on the record. This article's panel mechanism activates automatically once the lattice is large enough to support it.

## Article VI — Membership across the lattice

**6.1** A person's membership, standing, and contribution record belong to them within each deployment separately. Parties may agree mutually to recognise one another's contribution records or verification. The compact permits portability and never mandates it.

**6.2** No party may disclose to another party, or to anyone, what its own documents forbid disclosing. A member's identity protections travel with them.

## Article VII — Joining, leaving, amending

**7.1** A deployment joins by ratifying this compact through its own governance process and publishing that ratification in its record.

**7.2** Any party may leave at any time by the same process, without penalty. It keeps everything but the name, per Article IV.

**7.3** This compact amends by convention: any party may propose, and an amendment adopts when ratified by two-thirds of all parties, each through its own governance process. No amendment binds a party that has not ratified it. A party that declines a ratified amendment may remain under the prior text for one year, then must ratify or withdraw.

## Article VIII — Incorporating a deployment

**8.1** A deployment that incorporates in its own jurisdiction meets a problem worth naming once here rather than letting every party rediscover it. Incorporation statutes generally require a legal person to have **members** who elect its directors, and generally restrict that legal person from distributing its property to those same **members**. A deployment that pays a dividend to its participants and also makes those participants the corporation's members stands on both sides of that at once.

**8.2** The pattern this compact recommends is a **two-layer separation**. Governance stays whole in the deployment: every participant holds one vote on every binding decision, revocable liquid delegation, earned standing, a public contribution record, and both dividend tiers, exactly as the Founding Charter provides. Corporate membership is a separate and much smaller class — the holders of the deployment's elected Delegate Positions — who elect and remove the directors, and who take no dividend while serving, being compensated for the role instead under Charter 4.3. No member of the corporation receives a distribution, so the restriction has nothing to attach to. Participants are paid as participants, not as members.

**8.3** This is a pattern for incorporating, not a governance structure, and it is deliberately not part of the Founding Charter. A deployment must not read it as licence to insert an elected intermediary between its participants and its decisions.

**8.4** Nothing in this article is legal advice, and the shape of the constraint differs by jurisdiction. What generalises is the separation, not the clause numbers of any one statute. Each party is responsible for its own counsel, and 1.2 applies: nothing here grants any party or foundation authority over another's decisions.
## Closing

The lattice is the endgame the model has always implied: every level of governance, every kind of community, each independently running structures its own members chose — recognising each other, sharing what they build, and holding each other only to the floor that makes trust possible. Not one system ruling everything. Everything, ruled by its own.
