# Social campaign — structure and first 90 days

*The goal is not reach. The goal is thirty verified members, because that is the point at which the project stops being a document and starts producing evidence.*

---

## The one thing to hold onto

Every post ladders to a single question: **why is there no closed loop between a resident and the community they live in?**

Not "basic income is good," and not "elections are broken." Both arguments are saturated, polarised, and already have a thousand louder voices. Our angle is narrower and almost nobody is making it: **governance, contribution and civic return are built as three separate systems, and each one fails for reasons the other two would address.**

If a post doesn't serve that question, it doesn't go out.

---

## Five content pillars

**1. The open loop.** The core structural claim, told in ordinary language. What you put in doesn't come back. What comes back doesn't depend on what anyone puts in. And the decisions that affect you are unreachable between elections. This is the pillar everything else hangs from — use it to open, and return to it whenever a thread drifts.

**2. The pattern, wherever the audience lives.** A programme one administration introduces, the next can dismantle, regardless of whether it was working — and the people who depended on it have no standing to object until the next election. Almost every country has a version of this story. Let the audience supply their own example rather than importing ours; asking "what was yours?" outperforms telling them about someone else's.

**3. How it actually works.** Short, concrete mechanics. What delegation feels like. Taking your vote back mid-term instead of waiting four years. Delegating housing to one person and infrastructure to another. A public contribution record that earns standing and never buys an extra vote. One idea per post, no jargon, no clause numbers.

**4. Building in public.** Honest status updates, including the bad ones. "We found a rule in our own charter that would have capped the payment we were arguing for." That kind of post builds more trust than any polished claim, and it is genuinely what we have been doing.

**5. Where does it fail.** Direct invitations to argue. Post the weakest part of the model and ask people to break it. This is the project's actual voice and the most differentiated thing about it — almost nobody in this space invites attack.

Rough mix: 2 / 2 / 3 / 1 / 2 out of every ten posts.

## A note on scope

The organisation will incorporate in one country. The argument is not about that country, and the campaign should never read as though it is.

Write so that a reader in Lagos, Manila, Warsaw or Chicago recognises their own government in the description. Avoid national currencies, national programme names, and figures that only parse locally. Where a concrete example genuinely helps, reach for one from the reader's own context by asking rather than one from ours by telling.

This costs a little vividness and buys the only thing that matters at this stage: a person anywhere who is frustrated with their government can see themselves in it.

## Where to post

**Reddit is the highest-value channel and should be first.** Long-form is welcome, the audience argues in good faith when approached honestly, and the "tell me where this fails" framing is native to the platform rather than imported. Relevant communities exist for governance, civic technology, political philosophy, basic income, and local politics in most countries.

Rules for Reddit, and they matter more than the content: participate for two weeks before posting anything of our own. Never drop a link cold. Lead with the argument in the post body, link at the bottom if at all. Respond to every critical comment, especially the harsh ones — that is the content.

**Bluesky and X** for the short-form version: one idea, one image, one link. The charts work well here because they carry a claim on their own.

**LinkedIn** reaches the municipal, nonprofit and policy-adjacent audience — the people who could eventually host a shadow deployment. Different register: less provocation, more "here is a costed model and its published sources."

**Local and in-person** is underrated and probably where the first thirty actually come from. Library talks, community associations, municipal committee meetings. Thirty people is a room, not an audience.

---

## Cadence

Three posts a week is sustainable and enough. Two short, one substantial. One long-form piece a month — an essay, a build-in-public update, or a piece of analysis.

Do not launch everywhere at once. Start with Reddit and one short-form platform. Add others only once the first two have a rhythm.

---

## The conversion ladder

Nobody joins from a post. The path runs: **post → landing page → the proposition → join.**

Each step has one job. The post earns a click. The landing page earns five minutes. The proposition earns a decision. The join page is short and does not sell.

Measure the ladder, not the vanity numbers. Impressions mean nothing here. What matters is: how many reached the proposition, how long they stayed, how many joined. If reach is high and the argument page is empty, the hook is working and the substance is not.

## First 90 days

**Weeks 1–2 — presence without promotion.** Set up accounts. Participate in the communities as a person with opinions, not as a project. Post nothing of our own. This is not optional; skipping it is how good projects get flagged as spam.

**Weeks 3–4 — the open loop.** One long-form post on the strongest channel, built entirely around the structural claim: three systems, one missing loop. No pitch, no roadmap, no ask beyond "does this hold up?" Respond to everything.

**Weeks 5–8 — the mechanics.** Delegation first — it is the piece people find most novel once they understand it, and the easiest to demonstrate. Then the contribution record, then how return follows from both. Begin the first build-in-public updates.

**Weeks 9–12 — the ask.** Only now introduce "be one of the first thirty," and make the scarcity honest: thirty is a real threshold in the documents, not a marketing device. It activates the dividend and the dispute panel pool. Say so.

## Rules the campaign should not break

**Never overstate what exists.** The software runs, the documents are written, nothing is ratified and nobody has joined. Every post should be survivable by someone who then reads the whole site. The honesty is the brand — the moment a post oversells, the differentiator is gone.

**Never imply the model can do what only a legislature can.** Anything that would replace an existing public programme requires a government to legislate it. Say that plainly whenever it comes up, before a critic says it for us.

**No fabricated urgency.** The Rules of Procedure forbid the platform from manufacturing attention with countdowns, streaks and prompts. The marketing should hold to the same standard, or the first critic to notice the gap gets a free hit.

**Don't argue the politics.** When someone frames this as left or right, return to structure: the model doesn't decide what a community chooses, it decides whether the choice reflects the people living with it. Stay there.

**Answer the hostile comments first.** They are read by more people than the friendly ones, and answering them well is the single most persuasive thing available.

**Never name a municipality as a target.** Modelling a real city's budget in public, without a relationship there, reads as a takeover plan to anyone local. Use composites in anything public-facing until there is an actual conversation with someone in that city.

---

## What to make first

1. The landing page — done.
2. Five short chart posts, one per major claim, each standing alone without the site.
3. The long-form "open loop" post, written and held until the two-week participation window closes.
4. A one-page explainer for in-person conversations. Paper, not a link.
5. A pinned "where does it fail" thread on each platform, kept alive.
